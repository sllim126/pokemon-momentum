Yes, a PSA 1 can sell for more than a PSA 5 of the same Pokémon card. Some collectors want the 1 on the label, and a worn-out card in a slab can be harder to find than you'd think. But a price guide alone can't tell me how many buyers are waiting for one.

*I compared the November 23, 2026 [PriceCharting](https://www.pricecharting.com/category/pokemon-cards) estimates for the 4,677 Pokémon entries with positive prices for both Grade 1 and Grade 5. These grades include companies besides PSA; they are not a PSA-only comparison. The sample excludes cards missing either estimate.*

## How big can the difference be?

Here are two estimates from the same snapshot. Each link goes to the card's PriceCharting page:

- [Mega Charizard X ex #125, Phantasmal Flames](https://www.pricecharting.com/game/pokemon-phantasmal-flames/mega-charizard-x-ex-125): **$728 at Grade 1**, compared with **$242 at Grade 5**.
- [Charizard #4, Base Set](https://www.pricecharting.com/game/pokemon-base-set/charizard-4): **$361 at Grade 1**, compared with **$411 at Grade 5**.

The first card shows the premium; the second shows it isn't a rule. These are guide estimates, not a pair of verified sales or a promise that either card will sell at that price. Check the linked pages for current prices and the underlying sales.

Across the entries with both estimates, Grade 1 was higher for **3,859 out of 4,677**, or **83%**. The median Grade 1-to-Grade 5 ratio was **2.4**. That's the middle ratio in this particular sample, not a premium to apply to every card. Cards without a price for both grades are left out, so the result doesn't describe every Pokémon card.

## Why would anyone pay more for the worse grade?

A grade measures condition, but collectors don't all want the same condition. Some deliberately collect 1s: the low number is the point, and a card with a history of hard use can have its own appeal. A 5 doesn't occupy the same end of the scale.

Supply may matter too. If a damaged card isn't worth the cost of grading, its owner may leave it raw. Fewer submissions could mean fewer 1 slabs available to collectors who want them. That's a plausible explanation for a premium, not something the price file measures: it doesn't tell me population counts or why anyone bought a card.

## Should you grade a damaged card?

I'd start with recent sold listings for the exact card, printing, grade and grading company. Check how many actually sold and when, as well as what sellers are asking. A guide price based on thin sales can be a shaky target, and a mixed-company Grade 1 estimate isn't automatically the going rate for a PSA 1.

Then subtract grading, shipping, insurance and selling fees. Consider what the card could sell for ungraded, too. A damaged card won't necessarily receive a 1: [PSA's grading standards](https://www.psacard.com/gradingstandards) describe what it will grade and when a card may be rejected or given an authentic designation instead. I wouldn't damage a card to chase a number.
